A limit order book, and the tail that actually matters

A real price-time-priority matching engine, built from scratch, driving a live order book on the left. On the right: every operation timed and turned into a latency distribution. The average is a lie — the number that decides whether this system is good enough is the slowest 1 order in 1000.

An exchange is a queue. Buyers and sellers post the price they will accept, and the exchange pairs them off: best price first, and between two people offering the same price, whoever got there first. The part that decides whether such a system is usable is not how fast it usually is - it is how bad the occasional slow moment gets, because that is the moment a price moves while somebody is still waiting.

Latency distribution — the number that matters

Most orders are fast. The question that decides whether a matching engine is production-grade is not the average — it is how bad the slowest 1 in 1000 gets. The x-axis below is logarithmic because the interesting part of this distribution spans several orders of magnitude that a linear axis would crush into one spike.

Why "averaged over batches": browsers deliberately coarsen performance.now() (a side-channel mitigation) to a tick — often 0.1 ms — that a single order-book operation cannot clear. Timing one operation directly just measures the clock's own step function. So this page times batches of K operations with one performance.now() pair, divides by K, and takes one sample per batch — K is picked at runtime to comfortably clear the clock's measured resolution, shown below. The stats and chart are honestly labelled: they are per-operation cost averaged over each batch, with percentiles computed across batches — smoother than a true per-operation distribution, and unable to surface a genuine single-op outlier the way per-operation timing would if this clock allowed it.

Reading the labels: p50 is the middle - half of everything measured was faster than that. p99 means 99 out of every 100 were faster, and p99.9 means 999 out of every 1000 were. A microsecond (µs) is a millionth of a second. Here each measurement is one batch's average rather than one operation, for the reason above, so these are the middle and the tail of the batches.

Quoting the average here would hide the entire right-hand side of this chart, and the right-hand side is the part that costs money. Building the measurement so the tail survives it - and labelling honestly what the browser's clock will and will not let you see - is the work.
