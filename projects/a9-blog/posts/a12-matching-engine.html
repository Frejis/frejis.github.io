The average is a lie, the tail is the system

8 minute read order book · price-time priority · latency percentiles

Mean latency is the number everyone reports and nobody is hurt by. The slowest one operation in a thousand is the one that ruins the day, and it takes a log-scale histogram to even see it.

Open the demo

1The problem

Two things about order matching are hard to convey without showing them. The first is that "match buyers with sellers" hides a strict fairness rule — best price first, and within a price, strictly in arrival order — that an implementation can break in ways no casual test notices. The second is that performance here is not one number: quote a mean latency and you have described the operations nobody was waiting on.

Tail latency is where the behaviour actually lives, and it deserves a picture rather than a claim. A histogram on a linear axis will not give you one: matching latency spans several orders of magnitude, so everything collapses into a single spike at the left edge with the interesting part invisible off to the right.

2What I built

On the left, a working limit order book: sells stacked above buys, the spread highlighted between them, quantity bars per price level, and a trade tape. Submit a limit order, a market order or a cancel and the ladder moves.

On the right is the reason the project exists. Press "Run 50,000 orders" and a seeded stream of plausible order flow — mostly passive orders resting near the touch, some aggressive ones that cross the spread, some cancels — runs through the same engine with every operation timed by performance.now(). The result is a latency histogram on a logarithmic x-axis with p50, p99 and p99.9 drawn as labelled vertical lines. The distance between the first line and the last is the whole argument.

3How it works

Each side of the book is an array of price levels kept sorted by binary-search insert: bids descending, asks ascending, so index 0 is always the best price on that side. Matching an incoming order repeatedly takes levels[0] of the opposite side, checks it crosses, and fills against it until one runs out. Best-price priority is structural rather than something the matching code has to remember.

Within a level, resting orders sit in a plain array in arrival order and are never reordered. Time priority comes from a head pointer that advances past orders which are fully filled or cancelled, leaving them in place. This is what the data structure is chosen for: splicing a cancelled order out of the middle would shift every order behind it, silently renumbering their queue positions — precisely the bug price-time priority cannot tolerate. Skipping rather than removing is also amortised O(1), since each order is skipped once ever.

Measurement lives in latency.js. LatencyRecorder writes samples by index into a preallocated Float64Array rather than pushing onto a growing array, because a backing-store reallocation, or a garbage collection it triggers, landing inside the timed operation perturbs the exact thing you are measuring. Percentiles come from a sorted copy, never the live buffer, using linear interpolation at rank = p × (n − 1) — the convention NumPy's default and most APM tools use, documented in the source because percentile conventions disagree at the edges and silently produce different numbers. On a thousand samples, p99.9 is not "index 999", which would be the maximum; it is 99.9 per cent of the way from sample 998 to sample 999. A test pins that against a known 1..1000 array.

The chart buckets samples evenly in log space between the smallest positive sample and the largest, and places the percentile markers through the same log transform as the axis rather than as a fraction of a linear range. Getting that wrong is how a log chart ends up with correct bars and markers in the wrong place.

Correctness is held down by two invariants checked over a 20,000-action seeded run rather than by example tests alone. Conservation: every trade fills exactly one buyer and one seller by the same quantity, and no order ever fills past its original size. Never-crossed: after every operation, the best bid stays strictly below the best ask. Both are the kind of property that survives a hundred hand-written cases and then fails under sustained random pressure.

4What was hard

Measuring without the measurement lying. It is easy to write a benchmark whose own bookkeeping dominates what it reports, and the failure is invisible — you get plausible numbers, just not numbers about the thing you meant. The preallocated buffer, the copy before sorting, and keeping the timed region to the apply step alone are all in service of that.

The percentile convention was the other place to slow down. Two reasonable implementations of p99.9 disagree everywhere except p50 on an odd-length array, and neither is wrong — they are different definitions. Picking one, writing down which and why, and testing it against a hand-checkable array was cheaper than discovering later that the chart and the summary table were computed by different rules.

Cancellation shaped the data structure. Everything else about a price level would be simpler with a removal, and a removal is exactly what the fairness rule forbids.

5What it is not

  • Not a production matching engine. Limit and market orders only — no stop, iceberg or pegged orders, no risk checks, no persistence, no multi-instrument book, no network.
  • The timings are a browser's, not an exchange's. performance.now() in a browser tab measures this implementation's own cost with JIT warm-up and GC pauses from the rest of the page mixed in. It demonstrates the methodology honestly; it is not comparable to a co-located C++ engine.
  • The order flow is a caricature. Its mix of passive, aggressive and cancel actions is hand-picked to produce a realistic-looking book, not fitted to real market microstructure data.
  • Levels are a sorted array with binary-search insert, not the hash map plus ordered index, or skip list, a real book uses at higher order rates — the right trade-off at this scale, not a venue's.

It is the matching and measurement core in isolation. The interesting output is the shape of the histogram, not the numbers on its axis.
