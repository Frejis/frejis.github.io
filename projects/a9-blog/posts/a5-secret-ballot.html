Writeup · A5

Votes counted without ever being read

8 minute read Paillier · homomorphic encryption · BigInt

Homomorphic encryption is usually explained with a metaphor about gloveboxes. This one is explained with the actual integers.

Open the demo

1The problem

Computing on encrypted data sounds like a contradiction, and most people meet it as one. The usual explanation involves a locked box with gloves attached, which conveys the shape of the idea and none of the reason to believe it. Nothing in the metaphor tells you why the arithmetic works, and a reader who has been shown only a metaphor is right to remain sceptical.

Electronic voting is the natural setting, because the requirement is stark: the count must be correct and public, and no individual vote may be readable by anyone, including the people running the election. Those two demands look incompatible until you have seen the mechanism.

2What I built

A poll with a public bulletin board. Cast a vote and a real Paillier ciphertext appears on the board: a very large integer, encrypted in your browser. Anyone can read the board. Nobody can read a vote from it.

Then press "tally without decrypting". The page multiplies the ciphertexts together, one ballot at a time, visibly, and only at the very end decrypts the four running totals. A panel at the top of the page shows the mechanism directly with real numbers rather than a diagram: decrypt(encrypt(a) × encrypt(b) mod n²) = a + b. There is also a corrupt-then-verify demo: alter one ballot on the board and verification of the announced result fails.

3How it works

Paillier is additively homomorphic. Key generation picks two primes p and q, sets n = pq, takes g = n+1, λ = lcm(p−1, q−1) and μ = λ⁻¹ mod n. Encryption of a message m draws a random r and computes c = gm · rn mod n². Decryption applies L(cλ mod n²) · μ mod n, where L(x) = (x−1)/n.

The homomorphism falls straight out of that form. Multiplying two ciphertexts multiplies gm₁·r₁n by gm₂·r₂n, giving gm₁+m₂·(r₁r₂)n, which is exactly a valid encryption of m₁ + m₂ under the randomness r₁r₂. Multiplication in the ciphertext space is addition in the plaintext space, and the tally is nothing more than a product taken mod n².

All of that is implemented here from primitives rather than imported. BigInt arithmetic throughout: modular exponentiation by repeated squaring, modular inverse by the extended Euclidean algorithm, Miller-Rabin primality testing, and prime generation seeded from crypto.getRandomValues. The tests check Miller-Rabin against known primes and known composites including two Carmichael numbers, which defeat weaker primality tests, and they verify that encrypting the same plaintext twice gives different ciphertexts, which is the semantic security property the random r provides.

One ballot design decision is worth spelling out. Each ballot here is one ciphertext per option, each encrypting 0 or 1. The more common production design packs the whole ballot into a single ciphertext as m = Σ Bⁱ for a base B larger than the voter count, then extracts the per-option counts as digits of the decrypted total. Packing costs one ciphertext per ballot instead of one per option, which is strictly better engineering — and it turns every number on the board into a digit-extraction puzzle. For a page that has to be understood in ninety seconds, legible 0-or-1 encryptions win, at a cost of O(options) ciphertexts and homomorphic multiplications per ballot.

4What was hard

Making prime generation fast enough to be interactive without making it wrong. Key generation is a rejection loop: draw a random odd number of the requested bit length with the top bit set, test it, repeat. The test has to be Miller-Rabin with enough rounds to be trustworthy, and each round is a modular exponentiation on BigInts, so the naive version makes the page hang while the user waits with no explanation. The demo defaults to a 512-bit modulus for that reason, and says so on screen rather than quietly using a toy size and letting a reader assume otherwise. The benchmark panel measures 256, 512 and 1024 bits live with performance.now(), so the cost of the honest answer is visible too.

The second difficulty was resisting the urge to make the demo look more finished than it is. A tally that comes out right is not evidence the system is secure, and it would have been easy to present it as one. Hence the corrupt-then-verify button and a long, specific list of the guarantees that are missing.

5What it is not

  • No proof that a ballot is well formed. A malicious voter's browser could encrypt 1000, or a negative number, in any slot, and nothing here would catch it. Real systems attach a zero-knowledge range proof to every ballot.
  • No threshold decryption. One authority holds the whole private key. A real deployment splits it across trustees so no single party can decrypt alone.
  • No voter authentication. Anyone can type an alias and cast as many ballots as they like.
  • No coercion resistance or receipt-freeness. A voter can prove to a third party which ciphertext was theirs and what it contains.
  • Small modulus, in-memory board. 512 bits is a toy size chosen for interactivity; real deployments need 2048 or more. The bulletin board is a JavaScript array in one tab, not a distributed, append-only, publicly auditable log.

It is a correct implementation of additively homomorphic encryption and the tallying trick it enables. It is not a system anyone should run an election on.
