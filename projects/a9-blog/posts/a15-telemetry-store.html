One bit per timestamp, and the number I did not get

8 minute read Gorilla compression · time series · counter resets

A network that streams telemetry from every port produces a firehose of numbers that barely change. The interesting engineering is in not storing the part that did not change, and in the two bugs that show up once you do.

Open the demo

1The problem

Streaming telemetry from network hardware is a stream of samples that are almost, but not quite, the previous sample. A port reports every thirty seconds, forever; the timestamps are spaced identically, the counters step by similar amounts, the optical light level sits on one reading for minutes. Stored the obvious way — eight bytes of timestamp and eight of double per sample — almost all of that pays full price for information already known.

Then the operational half. A counter that only ever increases is the easiest thing in monitoring to compute a rate from, right up to the moment the device reboots and it starts again near zero. And a detector meant to catch a degrading link has to do it without crying wolf on a healthy one.

2What I built

A time-series database for a simulated fibre access network: OLTs with several ports each, streaming rx/tx counters, optical light level, temperature and CRC error counts every thirty seconds. On top of it, a from-scratch implementation of the Gorilla compression scheme from Pelkonen et al., "Gorilla: A Fast, Scalable, In-Memory Time Series Database", VLDB 2015, the ancestor of Prometheus's chunk encoding and InfluxDB's TSM engine.

The headline panel pushes a fleet's worth of samples through the compressor and reports what it measured, never a figure from the paper — quoting the paper's ratio would have been one line and looked three times better. Below it: live charts with a time-window selector that exercises downsampling, a label-matcher query panel, and a fault panel to degrade optics, burst CRC errors, or reboot a node.

3How it works

Timestamps are stored as delta-of-delta, the second derivative of the time axis, under the paper's bucket ladder: a single 0 bit when the delta-of-delta is zero, then 10 plus 7 bits, 110 plus 9 bits, 1110 plus 12 bits, and 1111 plus a raw 32 bits as the escape. A regular interval has a constant delta and therefore a zero delta-of-delta every time, so it costs exactly one bit per timestamp. compress.test.js asserts the encoded bit length against hand-computed arithmetic — header, first delta, one bit each thereafter — rather than only asserting a round trip, which would pass happily if every timestamp cost forty bits.

Values are XORed against their predecessor and stored as a leading-zero count, a meaningful-bit count and the meaningful bits, with the paper's window-reuse optimisation: when the previous block's window still covers this XOR, reuse it for two control bits. All of it runs on BigInt bit I/O, since a millisecond timestamp needs about 41 bits and a float64 pattern all 64, and JavaScript's bitwise operators truncate to 32.

One deviation from the paper is documented in the source. It states its bucket ranges inclusively as [-63, 64], [-255, 256], [-2047, 2048], one more positive value than a two's-complement field of that width holds; storing the value directly in two's complement, as this decoder reads it, gives -64..63, -256..255, -2048..2047. The cost is an escape to the 32-bit tier at exactly three values across the ladder, cheaper than a special case in both encoder and decoder.

The measured result on this project's generated telemetry: 3.43 bytes per sample, 27.4 bits per sample, 21.4 per cent of the naive 16-byte baseline. 100.0 per cent of timestamps cost exactly one bit; 39.3 per cent of values XORed to zero. That is materially worse than the paper's famous ~1.4 bytes per sample, and the reason is the data, not the codec: the simulation averages five metric types, including noisy floats like temperature and light level that change by a genuinely new amount most ticks, where Facebook's series were dominated by counters that barely move. These figures are from one run of the generator on this data.

Rate calculation treats any decrease in a counter as a reset and counts only the post-reset increase, the convention Prometheus's rate() uses, since a real counter never legitimately runs backwards. The demo puts the naive calculation beside it after you reboot a node, so the bug is visible rather than described: across the reset the naive version reports -1.997 billion, the reset-aware one 0. The anomaly detector on top is a rolling median plus a median absolute deviation scaled by 1.4826, window of 12, flagging at 5 sigma.

4What was hard

The detector, and not for the reason I expected. A first version flagged dozens of points on a clean, flat light-level series. The cause is quantisation: an optical power meter reports to 0.1 dB, so a quiet window is a run of identical values, the median absolute deviation of identical values is exactly zero, and dividing by it puts the smallest possible wobble infinitely many sigma from the median. The fix is a minDeviation floor at the sensor's own resolution: nothing below the step the instrument can resolve counts as deviation. Both directions are pinned by tests — it must fire on an injected degradation and stay silent on the baseline that produced the bug.

Worth noting how that bug was found: not by the test suite, which was green, but by opening the page and seeing a chart covered in markers with no business being there. A flat quantised signal is exactly the input a hand-written test case is least likely to contain, because it looks too boring to be a case.

5What it is not

  • Not a production time-series database. No durability, write-ahead log, compaction, clustering or replication — everything lives in memory for the lifetime of the tab.
  • The anomaly detector is a threshold heuristic, not machine learning. No concept of seasonality, trend, or simultaneous fault modes.
  • The telemetry is simulated. Not gNMI/OpenConfig or SNMP from real hardware — a caricature shaped like the metrics an OLT exports.
  • The compression figures are measured on this generated data, not a public dataset. Real hardware and metric mixes compress differently.

The motivation was a network specialist opening at Sinal in Silkeborg, which names streaming telemetry and time-series databases directly. I have not operated a Service Provider network; this is the storage-engine and telemetry engineering such a team depends on, built where it can be inspected.
