Writeup · A2

Convincing someone of a sum they never add up

8 minute read sumcheck · GKR · finite fields

My master's thesis implemented and benchmarked the GKR protocol. This is its inner loop, isolated and slowed down until you can watch it happen.

Open the demo

1The problem

A thesis line reading "implemented and benchmarked the GKR protocol" tells a reader almost nothing. Verifiable computation is a genuinely strange idea: a party that has done an enormous computation can convince you the answer is right, and checking the argument costs you far less than redoing the work. People either already know this and do not need convincing, or have never met it and cannot be persuaded by a sentence. The claim sounds like it should be impossible.

The engine underneath it, sumcheck, is short enough to write on a postcard. What is not obvious from the postcard is why it is sound: why a prover who has lied cannot simply keep lying consistently.

2What I built

The page runs the protocol as a conversation, one round at a time, with the transcript accumulating as you press through it. A grid on the right is the boolean hypercube the sum is taken over; every round pins one variable to a random field element and the grid halves. Two bars underneath count the field operations each side has actually performed. The prover's bar doubles with each extra variable. The verifier's grows by four operations per round. That widening gap is the entire reason the protocol exists.

There is also a cheating prover. Pick a cheat, run the protocol, and watch every round check pass — and then fail at the final oracle query. A soundness experiment repeats that over many trials and reports the measured rejection rate, and a benchmark times verification on your machine rather than quoting mine.

3How it works

Everything happens in the prime field p = 231−1, with BigInt arithmetic and inverses computed by Fermat's little theorem (ap−2). The object being summed is a multilinear extension: a table of 2v values at the boolean points, extended to the whole field so that it stays degree one in every variable. The tests check both halves of that: the extension agrees with the table at every boolean point, and it is linear along each axis.

The prover claims a value H for the sum over the hypercube. In round i it sends the univariate polynomial si(X) obtained by summing over all remaining variables with variable i left free. Because the polynomial is multilinear, si has degree one, so two evaluations si(0) and si(1) describe it completely. The verifier checks only that si(0) + si(1) equals the claim it is currently holding, picks a random ri, and takes si(ri) as its new claim. After v rounds every variable is bound, and the verifier makes one query to an oracle for g(r1..rv) and compares. Four operations a round, one evaluation at the end, against a sum the prover had to walk in full.

Soundness rests on the Schwartz-Zippel lemma. If the prover's claim is wrong, then at some round the polynomial it sends must differ from the true one; two distinct polynomials of degree d agree on at most d points, so a uniformly random ri catches the discrepancy except with probability d/p, and over v rounds the failure probability is at most d·v/p. The lie is not detected where it was told, it is pushed forward until it collides with the one honest value at the end.

4What was hard

The mathematics is short, so the work was elsewhere. The hardest part was building a cheating prover worth watching. A naive "corrupt one number in the table" toggle fails the round-one check immediately and teaches nothing, because it makes the protocol look like a checksum. Both cheats here are constructed to pass every individual round check: the lying prover patches s(1) so each round still sums to the previous claim, and the round-corrupter shifts s(0) and s(1) by equal and opposite deltas so their sum is preserved exactly. Neither can be caught by the local check. Both are caught by the final query. That is what soundness actually looks like, and it took some care to construct an adversary that consistent.

Two smaller ones. The operation counter is threaded through the field arithmetic itself rather than estimated afterwards, so the bars cannot drift out of agreement with what the code did. And a single verification is far under the resolution of a browser clock, so the benchmark replays 500 verifications and divides.

5What it is not

  • Interactive, not non-interactive. The verifier draws fresh randomness each round. There is no Fiat-Shamir transform, so nothing on the page is a proof you could publish.
  • Not zero-knowledge. The verifier learns the round polynomials and one evaluation of g. Sumcheck proves correctness, not secrecy.
  • No polynomial commitment. The final step is modelled as an oracle query. In a real system that is replaced by a commitment scheme, which is usually where most of the cost lives.
  • A small field on purpose. p = 231−1 keeps the numbers readable and makes the d·v/p bound weak by production standards. Real deployments use 128 bits or more, or repeat over an extension field.
  • One multilinear polynomial, not the layered circuit of full GKR. This is the inner loop, isolated so it can be watched.
