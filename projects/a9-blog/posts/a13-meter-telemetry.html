Writeup · A13

Every bit costs battery, and some corruption gets through

8 minute read bit packing · CRC-16 · power budgets

A water meter sealed in a basement has one battery and a decade or more to spend it. That decides the wire format, and the wire format decides what a checksum can save you from.

Open the demo

1The problem

Most software is written as though bytes are free, and for most software they are. Embedded telemetry is where that stops working. A utility meter is installed once, sealed, and expected to report for over a decade on a single cell, and the radio is by far the most expensive thing it does. Every bit is airtime, and airtime is milliamp-hours you do not get back.

So the reading gets packed at the bit width each field actually needs — and that opens the second problem. Once a field is eleven bits wide and starts partway through a byte, encoder and decoder have to agree on the layout exactly, or a full-value field bleeds a bit into its neighbour and nothing complains. And the third: the checksum catches most corruption, and the fraction it misses is a number an engineer should be able to quote.

2What I built

A telemetry frame codec in the spirit of wireless M-Bus, with a page built around four views of it. At the top, a byte map of a live frame where every bit is coloured by the field it belongs to; move a slider and the bits change. Below that, the same reading as a full frame and as a delta, side by side with their sizes.

Then the corruption playground: click any bit in the byte map to flip it and watch the CRC and the decode fail, or hand the frame to a seeded noisy channel and get back an exact three-way count of frames intact, frames the CRC caught, and frames corrupted that passed anyway. Last, a battery model turning frame size and transmit interval into years before somebody drives out to replace the meter.

3How it works

BitWriter and BitReader in frame.js move bits most-significant-first straight across byte boundaries, so a field's width is not constrained by alignment. A full frame carries a 1-bit frame type, a 24-bit meter ID, a 32-bit timestamp, 24 bits of cumulative volume, an 11-bit flow rate, 5 bits of battery level and 5 alarm flag bits: 102 bits, padded to 13 bytes, plus a 2-byte CRC. A delta frame replaces the absolute timestamp and volume with a 16-bit and a 12-bit delta against the previous reading — 74 bits, 10 bytes, 12 with the CRC. Consecutive readings differ by very little, so most transmissions can be the small one. A delta too large for its field throws a RangeError rather than wrapping, because silent wraparound surfaces as an impossible figure on a customer's bill months later.

The checksum is CRC-16/CCITT-FALSE: polynomial 0x1021, init 0xFFFF, no reflection, no final XOR, computed bit by bit over the payload. Real wireless M-Bus uses CRC-16/EN13757, which reflects both input and output. I chose the non-reflected variant because its division is something you can read off the register and believe, where a reflected CRC hides the same arithmetic behind a bit-reversal that adds code and no understanding. It is a real, named CRC, checked in the tests against its published value: the CRC of the ASCII bytes "123456789" is 0x29B1.

The channel flips each bit independently at a chosen error rate, driven by a seeded mulberry32 generator so a run is reproducible. Each frame lands in exactly one of three buckets — unchanged, rejected by the CRC, or changed and accepted anyway — and a test asserts intact + caughtByCrc + undetected === total exactly. The third bucket is the number that matters: a 16-bit CRC misses roughly one corrupted frame in 65,536, so over thousands of frames at a few per cent bit error rate, a handful get through with a valid checksum and wrong data.

The power model is arithmetic on stated constants. Airtime per frame is a fixed wake-and-settle overhead plus bytes×8 over the bit rate; multiply by transmit current and transmissions per year, add the sleep draw, divide cell capacity by the total. That overhead is why a smaller payload helps less than its byte count suggests.

4What was hard

Bit-level field layout looks trivial and is not. Get one shift or one field order wrong and the frame still encodes, still decodes, and is wrong only for certain values. The test that pins it puts every field at its maximum at once and checks each one afterwards — the condition under which a boundary bug bleeds into the next field. The suite is strong enough to catch a decoder reading a different width than the encoder wrote, and a CRC initialised with the wrong variant's constant.

The undetected-corruption count took discipline rather than cleverness. It is easy to build a corruption demo that shows the CRC catching errors and never asks about the ones it misses; that version looks better and teaches less. Counting all three outcomes makes the blind spot a fact rather than a footnote.

The battery model needed the opposite restraint. It is the most impressive-looking output on the page and the least trustworthy, so it is presented as an order of magnitude responding sensibly to frame size and interval, not a figure to sign off.

5What it is not

  • Not real wireless M-Bus. The field layout, frame structure and CRC polynomial are this project's own design, in the spirit of that protocol family rather than compliant with it. Do not point a real meter reader at these frames.
  • The power model is illustrative. Fixed wake overhead, flat transmit current, flat sleep current, one bit rate. Real hardware has ramp-up curves, temperature dependence, self-discharge and duty-cycle rules that none of this touches.
  • The channel is i.i.d. bit flips. Real radio channels produce correlated bursts of errors, not independent per-bit ones — enough to make the blind spot visible, not to model a link budget.
  • No link layer around the frame. No addressing beyond the meter ID, no encryption, no acknowledgement or retransmission.

Every constant on the page is a named, overridable field in power.js and channel.js. Nothing shown is a number typed into a document and never checked against the code behind it.
