Meter Telemetry

A bit-packed radio frame for a smart utility meter, in the style of wireless M-Bus: every field laid out by hand at the bit width it actually needs, checked with a CRC, and paid for out of a battery with a design goal of surviving well over a decade in the field on one cell. Each transmission costs battery. Smaller frames mean fewer years spent driving out to replace meters.

1 · The frame, bit by bit

Every field below is packed at exactly the bits it needs — an 11-bit flow rate, a 5-bit battery percentage, single-bit alarm flags — not rounded up to the nearest byte. Change a reading and watch the exact bits change. This is the whole demo in one picture.

2 · Full frame vs delta frame

A full frame states every field from scratch. A delta frame states the change since the previous reading, which is almost always a much smaller number. Both are the same code, run twice, on the reading above.

Run many frames at a bit error rate

  

    Most bit errors are caught by the CRC and the frame is simply dropped.
    The number that matters to an embedded engineer is the other one: how
    many corrupted frames pass the CRC anyway and get treated as real data.
    A 16-bit CRC misses roughly 1 in 65,536 (2-16) corrupted
    frames, so seeing one takes tens of thousands of corrupted frames, not
    just of frames sent — each run below adds to the running total so the
    undetected count actually has a chance to show up.
  

  

    
    
    
    
    
  

  

  

  






  
4 · The battery budget

  

    Transmitting costs milliamps for a fraction of a second; sleeping costs
    microamps for the rest of the day. How often the meter reports, and how
    many bytes it sends each time, are the two numbers that decide whether a
    site visit happens in five years or in twenty.

        At a short interval the payload dominates, so a smaller delta frame
        buys real years. Stretch the interval far enough and transmissions
        become rare — the battery is then limited by self-discharge, which
        does not care how many bytes you send, so the delta advantage
        shrinks towards ×1.00. That collapse is the self-discharge floor
        showing up, not a bug in the comparison.
